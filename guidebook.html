chapter 1 · the pawn

meet the pawn

The pawn is the smallest piece in chess, but there are a lot of them! Each player starts with 8 pawns lined up in front of all the other pieces.

In chess we count pieces like points. A pawn is worth 1 point.

💡 Pawns only ever move forward, never backwards!
chapter 1 · the pawn

moving forward

A pawn walks straight forward, one square at a time.

On its very first move, a pawn may jump two squares instead if it wants to.

💡 A pawn can't move backwards or sideways.
chapter 1 · the pawn

capturing is different!

Pawns move straight, but they capture diagonally: one square forward and to the left or right.

A pawn cannot capture a piece directly in front of it. If something blocks the way, the pawn is stuck.

chapter 1 · the pawn

promotion ⭐

If a pawn makes it all the way to the other side of the board, it gets promoted: it turns into a stronger piece!

Most players choose a queen, the strongest piece. In amin's chess, your pawn becomes a queen automatically.

💡 Even a little pawn can be powerful. Protect your pawns that are close to the end!
chapter 1 · the pawn

en passant

It sounds fancy (say "on pa-SAH") and it's the pawn's secret move!

If an enemy pawn jumps two squares and lands right next to your pawn, you can capture it as if it had only moved one square.

But you must do it on your very next move, or the chance is gone.

chapter 1 · the pawn

quick recap

  • Each player has 8 pawns, worth 1 point each
  • Pawns move forward one square (two on their first move)
  • Pawns capture one square diagonally forward
  • A piece directly in front blocks a pawn
  • Reach the far end and a pawn is promoted
  • En passant: a special pawn-only capture

chapter 2 · the knight

meet the knight

The knight looks like a horse! Each player has 2 knights, standing next to the rooks. A knight is worth 3 points.

It moves in an L shape: two squares in one direction, then one square to the side.

💡 Think "two steps, then turn": 2 + 1.
chapter 2 · the knight

the jumper

The knight is the only piece that can jump over other pieces, both its own and the enemy's.

It captures by landing on an enemy piece.

💡 A knight can't land on a square that has one of its own pieces.
chapter 2 · the knight

quick recap

  • 2 knights each, worth 3 points
  • Moves in an L: two squares, then one to the side
  • The only piece that can jump over others
  • Captures by landing on an enemy piece
  • Great in the center and early in the game

chapter 3 · the bishop

meet the bishop

Each player has 2 bishops, standing next to the king and queen. A bishop is worth 3 points.

Bishops move diagonally, as many squares as they like, as long as nothing is in the way.

chapter 3 · the bishop

one color forever

A bishop always stays on the same color squares. The bishop that starts on a dark square only ever visits dark squares. That's why every player gets one of each!

💡 Bishops can't jump. They stop before their own pieces, or capture enemy ones.
chapter 3 · the bishop

quick recap

  • 2 bishops each, worth 3 points
  • Moves diagonally, any number of squares
  • Stays on one color of square for the whole game
  • Can't jump over pieces
  • Strongest when the diagonals are open

chapter 4 · the rook

meet the rook

Rooks look like castle towers and start in the corners. Each player has 2 rooks, worth 5 points each.

Rooks move in straight lines: forward, backward, left or right, as far as they like.

chapter 4 · the rook

blocked or capturing

Rooks can't jump. They stop before their own pieces, and they can capture the first enemy piece in their path.

💡 Rooks love open files: columns with no pawns in the way. They also team up with the king in a special move called castling.
chapter 4 · the rook

quick recap

  • 2 rooks each, worth 5 points
  • Moves in straight lines, any number of squares
  • Can't jump over pieces
  • Captures the first enemy piece in its path
  • Works with the king to castle

chapter 5 · the queen

meet the queen

The queen is the strongest piece, worth 9 points. Each player has just one.

She moves like a rook and a bishop combined: any number of squares in a straight line or on a diagonal.

chapter 5 · the queen

power, with care

The queen can capture along any of her lines, but she can't jump over pieces.

💡 Losing your queen early is a big problem. Bring out knights and bishops first, and keep your queen protected.

  • Moves like a rook and a bishop together
  • Can't jump over pieces
  • The most powerful piece, so protect her
  • Starts on the square of her own color

chapter 6 · the king

meet the king

The king is the most important piece. If your king is trapped, you lose the game!

He moves one square in any direction.

💡 A king can never move to a square where an enemy piece could capture him.
chapter 6 · the king

check!

When a king is under attack, it's called check. You must fix it right away!

  • Move the king to a safe square
  • Block the attack with another piece
  • Capture the attacking piece
chapter 6 · the king

checkmate!

Checkmate means the king is in check and has no way out. The game ends and the player who delivered it wins! 🎉

💡 If a player has no legal move but is not in check, it's a draw called stalemate.
chapter 6 · the king

castling

Castling is a special move that tucks your king away safely and wakes up a rook. The king slides two squares toward a rook, and the rook hops over to the other side.

💡 You can only castle if the king and that rook haven't moved yet, nothing is between them, and the king isn't in check or passing through an attacked square. In amin's chess, just tap your king and pick the castling square!
chapter 6 · the king

quick recap

  • The king moves one square in any direction
  • Lose your king and you lose the game
  • Check: the king is attacked, so you must respond
  • Checkmate: check with no escape, game over
  • Castling: a king and rook special move

chapter 7 · the chessboard

64 squares

A chessboard is a grid of 64 squares: 8 rows and 8 columns, in alternating light and dark colors.

If you're playing on a physical board, always set it up so there's a light square in the bottom-right corner, for both players!

💡 Remember: "light on the right".
chapter 7 · the chessboard

files and ranks

The columns going up and down are called files. They have letters, a to h.

The rows going side to side are called ranks. They have numbers, 1 to 8.

💡 Diagonals are the third kind of line. Bishops and queens love them!
chapter 7 · the chessboard

naming squares

Every square has its own name: its file letter followed by its rank number. That's how chess players write down moves!

💡 Say the letter first, then the number: "e4", never "4e".
chapter 7 · the chessboard

quick recap

  • The board has 64 squares, light on the right
  • Files are columns, named a to h
  • Ranks are rows, numbered 1 to 8
  • A square's name is its letter plus its number

chapter 8 · setting up

the starting position

Pawns fill the second row in front of each player. Behind them, the other pieces line up in this order: rook, knight, bishop, queen, king, bishop, knight, rook.

chapter 8 · setting up

queen on her color

The two kings and queens always face each other across the board. The trick to not mixing them up:

The queen starts on a square that matches her color. The white queen starts on a light square, and the black queen on a dark square.

💡 "Queen on her color" and "light on the right" are the two checks for a correct setup.
chapter 8 · setting up

white goes first

White always moves first. After that, the players take turns, one move each.

You can't skip a turn, and you can't move two pieces at once (except when castling).

💡 In amin's chess online games, you're given a color automatically. A rematch swaps colors!
chapter 8 · setting up

quick recap

  • Pawns go on the second row, the other pieces behind them
  • Back row: rook, knight, bishop, queen, king, bishop, knight, rook
  • Queen on her color
  • White moves first, then players take turns

chapter 9 · capturing and trading

how to capture

To capture, move your piece onto the square of an enemy piece The enemy piece is removed the board.

You can never capture your own pieces, and capturing is never forced unless it's the only way out of check.

chapter 9 · capturing and trading

piece values

Some pieces are worth more than others. Players count points to see who is ahead:

  • ♟︎ pawn: 1 point
  • ♞︎ knight: 3 points
  • ♝︎ bishop: 3 points
  • ♜︎ rook: 5 points
  • ♛︎ king: priceless!
chapter 9 · capturing and trading

good trades

A trade is when you capture a piece and your opponent captures one back. Count the points to see if it was fair:

✅ Knight for a bishop: even (3 for 3)
✅ Pawn takes a knight: you win 2 points
❌ Rook for a knight: you lose 2 points

💡 Before you capture, ask: "Can they capture me back?"
chapter 9 · capturing and trading

quick recap

  • Capture by moving onto an enemy piece
  • Pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9
  • A trade is a capture followed by a capture back
  • Count the points to tell good trades from bad ones

chapter 10 · how a game ends

checkmate wins

The most common way to win is checkmate: the enemy king is in check and can't escape.

Pieces work together to do it. Here, the queen gives check and the king protects her, so the black king can't take her.

chapter 10 · how a game ends

other ways to win

🏳️ Resignation: if a player knows they're losing, they can resign and the other player wins.

⏱️ Time: in timed games, a player whose clock runs out loses.

💡 Resigning isn't giving up on learning! Strong players resign when the game is clearly lost, then play again.
chapter 10 · how a game ends

draws

Sometimes nobody wins. That's a draw, and it happens when:

  • Stalemate: no legal moves, but not in check
  • Both players agree to a draw
  • The same position repeats three times
  • Neither side has enouugh pieces to checkmate
chapter 10 · how a game ends

quick recap

  • Checkmate: the king is attacked with no escape
  • A player can also win by resignation or time
  • Stalemate is a draw, not a win
  • Draws also come from agreement or repeating positions

opening ideas

chapter 11 · opening ideas

fight for the center

The four squares in the middle of the board, d4, e4, d5 and e5, are the most important. Pieces in the center can reach more of the board.

💡 Start with a pawn move like e4 or d4 to claim the center.
chapter 11 · opening ideas

develop your pieces

Bring your knights and bishops out from the back row early. This is called development, and it lets your pieces join the game.

💡 Try not to move the same piece twice in the opening.
chapter 11 · opening ideas

castle early

Once your knight and bishop are out of the way, castle! It tucks your king behind a wall of pawns and brings your rook toward the action.

chapter 11 · opening ideas

common mistakes

❌ Bringing the queen out too early. Enemy pieces can chase her around.

❌ Moving lots of pawns. Pieces win games, not just pawn moves.

❌ Forgetting your opponent. Always ask what their last move threatens.

💡 Fix all three with the plan: center, develop, castle.
chapter 11 · opening ideas

quick recap

  • Fight for the center squares
  • Develop your knights and bishops
  • Castle to protect your king
  • Keep the queen at home early on
  • What what your opponent is threatinging

chapter 12 · staying safe

attacked or defended

A piece is attacked when an enemy piece could capture it next move.

A piece is defended when one of your pieces could capture back if it's taken.

chapter 12 · staying safe

hanging pieces

A piece that is atttacked and not defended is called hangingg. Your opponent can capture it for free!

💡 Most beginner games are decided by who leaves more pieces hangiing.
chapter 12 · staying safe

three questions

Before every move, ask yourself:

  • What did my opponents last move do?
  • Are any of my pieces attacked?
  • Will my move leave something hanging?

This habit will save you more pieces than any trick!

chapter 12 · staying safe

quick recap

  • Attacked: an enemy could capture it
  • Defended: you could capture back
  • Hanging: attacked and not defended
  • Check the three questions before every move

chapter 13 · forks

what is a fork?

A fork is when one piece attacks two or more enemy pieces at the same time.

Your opponent can only save one of them, so you win the other!

💡 Forks are strongest when one target is the king, because the king has to move.
chapter 13 · forks

knight forks

Knights are the best forkers. They jump in an L, so their attacks are easy miss!

💡 Every move, look at the squares your knight can jump to. Does any of them attack two things?
chapter 13 · forks

pawn forks

Even a humble pawn can fork. A pawn attacks two squares diagonally, so if two enemy pieces stand on them, you win one.

💡 A pawn is only worth 1 point, so a pawn fork is a great deal!
chapter 13 · forks

quick recap

  • A fork attacks two or more pieces at once
  • Your opponent can only save one
  • Forking the king is extra strong
  • Check for forks before everyy move, both ways

chapter 14 · pins

A pin is when a piece can't move because it would expose a more valuable piece behind it.

If the piece behind is the king, the pinned piece is not allowed to move at all. That's an absolute pin.

chapter 14 · pins

the relative pin

If the piece behind is not the king, the pinned piece is allowed to move, but it wouuld cost you the more valuable piece behind it.

💡 Pins are made by bishops, rooks and queens, the pieces that move in long lines.
chapter 14 · pins

attack the pinned piece

A pinned piece can't run away, it's a sitting duck. Attack it with something les valuable, like a pawn.

escaping a pin

Stuck in a pin? You have options:

  • Capture the piece that is pinning
  • Chase it away with a pawn
  • Move the valuable piece behind (if it isnt the king)
  • Castle your king to safty
chapter 14 · pins

quik recap

  • A pin freezes a piece in front of a more valuable one
  • Pinned to the king: it cannot move at all
  • Pinned to something else: it can move but you lose material
  • Attack pinned pieces with pawns
  • Break pins by capturing, chasing or castling

what is a skewer?

A skewer is the opposite of a pin. The more valuable piece is in front, so it has to move out of the way, and you can capture the piece behind it.

bishop skewers

Rooks, bishops and queens can all skewer, because they attack along long lines

💡 Look for kings for queens that sit on the same file, rank or diagonal.

chapter 16 · discovered attacks

uncover a surprise

A discovered attack happens when you move one piece out of the way and uncover an attack from another piece hiding behind it.

Your opponent suddenly faces two threats at once!

chapter 16 · discovered attacks

discovered check

Even better: the uncovered attack is check! Your opponent must answer the check, so the piece you moved is free to do damage.

💡 Black has to deal with the check first, so White takes the queen next move.
chapter 16 · discovered attacks

double check

What if the moved piece gives check too? That's a double check. You can't block or capture two attackers at once, so the king has to move.

chapter 16 · discovered attacks

quick recap

  • Move a piece to uncover an attack from another
  • The moved piece can make its own threat at the same time
  • Discovered check forces your opponent to respond
  • Double check: only the king can move
  • Look for your pieces lind up behind each other

chapter 17 · back-rank mate

the trapped king

Beginners often keep their pawns in front of a castled king. That wall can turn a trap.

If a rook or queen checks along the back rank (the row behind the pawns), the king's own pawns block his escape. Thats called back-rank mate.

chapter 17 · back-rank mate

make some luft

The fix is simple: give your king and escape square. Chess players call this luft, which is German for "aor".

💡 Move the h-pawn (or g-pawn) one step early is a great habit.
chapter 17 · back-rank mate

your turn!

White to move. Can you find the checkmate? Look at the back rank!

chapter 17 · back-rank mate

quick recap

  • A back-rank mate checks the king along the first or last row
  • The king's own pawns block his escape
  • Make luft with a pawn move such as h3 or h6
  • Keep a rook on the back rank if you can
  • Look for it against your opponent too!
